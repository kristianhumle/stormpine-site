The pre-interview brief.

Walk into any conversation with the whole relationship and history already in your head, instead of reconstructing it from an email thread two minutes before the call.

7 min readUpdated Aug 2026For any live process

Senior conversations are decided in the first few minutes, and often by something small: whether you remember what was discussed last time, whether you follow up on the thing they mentioned in passing, whether you sound like someone who has been thinking about them rather than juggling ten identical calls.

None of that requires a better memory. It requires a brief: a short, deliberate reconstruction of the relationship, done just before you walk in.

Why the brief matters more at this level

Executive and board processes run for months, with long gaps between touchpoints. By the time you reach a second or third conversation, the details have faded: who introduced you, what they were worried about, what you promised to send. The other side notices when you have kept the thread, and they notice, unfavourably, when you have not.

A good brief closes that gap. It lets a six-month relationship feel continuous, which at this level reads as seriousness and respect.

What goes in a brief

Keep it to one screen. You are not preparing a dossier; you are reloading context. The five things worth having in front of you:

  • Who they are, and how you connected. The introducer, the first touchpoint, the mutual context.
  • What was said last time. The substance of the previous conversation, in a sentence or two.
  • What you promised. Anything you owe them, references, a portfolio, a follow-up thought, and whether you delivered it.
  • Where this actually stands. The real stage of the process, not the hopeful one.
  • The one thing you want from this call. A single, clear objective, so the conversation has a direction.

Where the content comes from

The brief is only as good as the record behind it. If your notes live in your head, the brief is a guess. The discipline that makes this effortless is capturing a two-line note after every meaningful interaction, while it is fresh: what was discussed, what you agreed, what to do next. Done consistently, the brief writes itself.

The most prepared person in the room is rarely the one who studied hardest. It is the one who kept a record, and spent five minutes reading it back.

The takeaway
  • ✓ Reconstruct the relationship, not just the role, before every conversation.
  • ✓ One screen: who, what was said, what you owe, where it stands, what you want.
  • ✓ Capture a two-line note after each interaction, so the brief is never a guess.
