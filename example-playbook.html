Running three searches at once without dropping a thread.

The hardest part of a senior move is rarely any single conversation. It is holding four or five of them, at different stages, across different firms, without letting the promising one quietly stall while you chase the loud one.

Most advice treats a job search as one funnel: apply, interview, offer. At the executive level it is nothing like that. You are running several parallel processes at once, each with its own pace, its own gatekeeper, and its own set of unwritten rules, and none of them behaves like a funnel. Some are formal searches run by a firm. Some are a standing conversation with a chair that has quietly been going for a year. Some are a warm introduction that has not decided yet whether it is real.

The failure mode is never that you run out of energy for the search. It is subtler: a process you genuinely cared about goes quiet for three weeks, you are busy with the loud one, and by the time you notice, the moment to act on it has passed. Multiply that across five live threads and the good outcomes start leaking away without you ever deciding to let them go.

This playbook is the method for keeping every process visible, knowing exactly what each one is waiting on, and walking into every conversation with the whole thread already in your head. It is five habits. None of them is complicated; the discipline is in doing them every week.

What you'll take away
  • ✓ One view of every live process and the exact stage it sits at
  • ✓ A weekly read on what is moving, what is waiting on you, and what has gone quiet
  • ✓ A five-minute pre-call routine that replaces re-reading an email thread
  • ✓ A way to keep momentum without ever looking anxious to a search consultant
1

The parallel-process board

Before anything else, get all of it out of your inbox and your memory and into one place. Every opportunity becomes a single card: the role, the firm, the person who owns it for you, and the one stage it sits at right now. The point is not tidiness. It is that you can no longer forget a process exists, because forgetting is exactly what kills the ones that matter.

Keep the stages honest, and phrase them as reality rather than hope. "Waiting to hear back" is not a stage; it is a feeling. "Second interview held on the 9th, they owe me next steps" is a stage, because it tells you what is true and what should happen next. The difference sounds pedantic until you have fifteen cards, at which point it is the whole game.

One board, one glance, every process accounted for. If a card has not moved and you cannot say why, that is not an oversight to feel guilty about, it is the single most useful signal on the board, and step two is what you do with it.

2

Reading momentum

Once a week, deliberately, sort every process into three buckets: moving, waiting on you, and gone quiet. Most people only ever look at the moving ones, because they feel good and they flatter the effort. The entire value of the exercise is in the other two columns.

Anything waiting on you is a promise you have not kept: a reference you owe, a portfolio you said you would send, an introduction you offered to make. Clear those first, always, before you do anything else. Nothing damages standing at this level faster than being the bottleneck in a process you claim to want.

Anything gone quiet needs a judgement, and it is the judgement that separates a good operator from an anxious one: is this a stall, or a soft no? A stall is a process where the other side still wants to move but something slipped, a hiring manager on holiday, a board meeting that pushed a decision, a quarter-end that swallowed everyone. A soft no is a process that has actually ended, but politely, without anyone doing you the courtesy of saying so.

The tell is almost always the introducer or the owner. A warm, specific reply to a light, low-stakes nudge means it is a stall, and worth patient re-engagement. A vague, delayed, or absent reply, especially from someone who was previously responsive, means it is a no, and your energy belongs on the other cards. Reading this correctly, and not flattering yourself about which it is, is the highest-value skill in a parallel search.

3

The pre-call brief

Before any conversation, spend five minutes reconstructing the whole thread. Who is this person, and how did you meet? What was said last time? What did you promise, and did you deliver it? Where does this actually stand? And the one thing you want out of this specific call?

This matters more at senior level than anywhere else, because these processes run for months with long gaps between touchpoints. By your third conversation with a chair, the details have faded: who introduced you, what they were quietly worried about, the aside they made that you meant to follow up on. Walking in cold, or worse, visibly scrolling an email while they talk, is precisely what makes an otherwise strong candidate forgettable.

Done well, the brief lets a six-month relationship feel continuous. You reference the thing they mentioned last spring; you close the loop you opened; you sound like someone who has been thinking about them specifically rather than juggling ten identical calls. At this level that reads as seriousness, and it compounds.

4

Managing the search consultant

A retained search consultant is not your agent, and treating them like one is a common and costly mistake. They are engaged by, and paid by, the hiring organisation. Their loyalty is to the client and to their own long-term reputation for presenting excellent, reliable candidates. Understand that, and you can build a genuinely valuable relationship on the right terms.

Be the candidate who is easy to represent: responsive, clear about what you want and what you do not, honest about your other processes when it is relevant, and never dramatic. Consultants remember the people who made them look good to a board, and those are the people they call first about the next mandate, the one that is not even open yet.

Cadence and candour are the levers. Give them enough to advocate for you accurately; hold back the things that are not theirs to know yet, such as the precise state of a competing offer, until sharing serves you. A good consultant relationship outlives any single role, so play it as the multi-year relationship it is, not as a transaction tied to one search.

5

When to press, when to wait

Momentum is a tempo, not a volume. Pressing every process every week reads as anxious, lowers your standing, and trains the other side to see you as available rather than sought-after. Letting them all drift, on the other hand, reads as indifferent, and quietly hands the initiative to whoever follows up first.

The craft is a single, well-judged follow-up per process, timed to something real: after their board meeting, once their quarter closes, when the introducer is back in the room and can add a word. A nudge anchored to a real event is a courtesy and a signal of attentiveness. The same nudge sent for no reason is noise, and it costs you.

Hold the whole board in view and you can time each thread independently, pressing the one that is genuinely ripe while letting another sit exactly as long as it needs to. That is what running several searches at once actually looks like when it is done well: not more effort, but better-timed effort, spread deliberately across every live thread.

The candidate who wins a parallel search is rarely the most available. It is the one who always seems to have the whole picture, and never seems to be chasing.
